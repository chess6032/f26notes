# Points and Vectors

## Points & Coordinate systems

Points are described numerically by placing them in a coordinate system with an origin. But notice that the same point can be described in many different coordinate system.

Some coord sys are better for some things. e.g., most screens use a coordinate system where the $y$ axis points "down". Transitioning between coord sys requires a ***transformation*** (which we'll talk about shortly).

### $x$/$y$ vs. row/column

We often manipulate coords in $x$/$y$ and then store them in rows/cols. Remember that rows correspond to $y$ and cols to $x$.

## Vectors

### Points vs. Vectors

Both points and vectors can be represented by their cartesian coordinates&mdash;$(x_1, y_1)$ or $[x_1, y_1]^T$, respectively. For the vector representation, you're assuming that the tail is at the origin. However, they are not the same.

* Vectors do not store *location*. Two vectors with the same magnitude & direction are the same no matter where they're located.
* Points have neither direction nor magnitude.

### Common-use dimensions for vectors

- We'll usually use $\mathbb R^4$ vectors for 3D graphics.
- In some applications (including those beyond computer graphics), we'll represent a bunch of data as a multi-dimensional vector.

### Vectors as displacements

In CG, it's common to think of a vector as a "displacement" from the tail to the head. Or from a point to another point. e.g., in $\mathbf p_1 + \mathbf v = \mathbf p_2$, the vector $\mathbf v$ is the displacement from point $\mathbf p_1$ to $\mathbf p_2$.

> [!NOTE]
> $\mathbf v = \mathbf p_2 - \mathbf p_1$ gives you the vector that goes from $\mathbf p_2$ to $\mathbf p_1$.

### Geometric interpretation of vector addition

"Go this way, then go that way".

- The result of $\mathbf a + \mathbf b$ is the vector that walks from the tail of $\mathbf a$ to the head of $\mathbf b$. 
- The reuslt of $\mathbf c - \mathbf d$ is the vector that walks from the *head* of $\mathbf c$ to the *tail* of $\mathbf d$.

### Magnitude

```math
\lVert \mathbf v \rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v}
```


### Zero vector

```math
\mathbf 0 = \begin{bmatrix}
0 \\ 
0 \\
\vdots \\
0
\end{bmatrix}
```

* Has no direction, no magnitude.
* Orthogonal to all vectors.

### Unit vector / normalization

A lot of operations in CG assume your vectors are unit vectors.

To find a unit vector in the same direction of some vector $\mathbf v$, divide $\mathbf v$ by its magnitude, $\lVert \mathbf v \rVert$. This is called ***normalization***.

```math
\hat {\mathbf v} = \frac {\mathbf v} {\lVert \mathbf v \rVert}
```

### Geometric interpretation of dot products

#### Dotting with unit vectors

The dot product of a vector onto a *unit* vector is the length of the projection onto that unit vector.

```math
\mathbf v \cdot \hat {\mathbf u} = \lVert \text{proj}_{\hat {\mathbf u}} \mathbf v \rVert
```

#### Sign of dot product

The sign of a dot product tells you whether the "projection is in the same direction".

- $\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b < 0$: angle is *greater* than 90 deg
- $\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b = 0$: angle *is* 90 deg
- $\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b > 0$: angle is *less* than 90 deg

#### Angles

```math
\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \lVert \mathbf u \rVert \lVert\mathbf v \rVert (\hat {\mathbf u} \cdot \hat {\mathbf v}) \\
= \lVert \mathbf u \rVert \lVert\mathbf v \rVert \cos(\theta)_{uv}
```

### Properties of dot product

- Commutative.
- Distributes over vector addition.


```math
\mathbf a \cdot (\mathbf b + \mathbf c) = \mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b + \mathbf a \cdot \mathbf c
```

### Geometric interpretation of cross product

$\mathbf u \times \mathbf v$ produces a vector orthogonal to *both* $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$.

Cross product also encodes the $\sin$ between two vectors.

# Transformations

Transformations are how you change coordinate systems.

ALL transformations are done via mtx mult.

## Translation

- Translating a coordinate system simply **moves its origin**.

```math
\mathbf p' = \mathbf p + \mathbf t \\
\text{where } \mathbf t \text{ is the translation}
```

## Rotation

- Rotation a coordinate system "turns" its axes.
- To compute coordinate into the rotated system, project each of the new axis directions.
  - ts uses dot prods.

```math
p'_x = \mathbf p \cdot \hat {\mathbf e_x} \\
p'_y = \mathbf p \cdot \hat {\mathbf e_y} \\
```

## Matrices

An $n \times m$ matrix has $n$ *rows* (vertical) and $m$ *columns* (horizontal).

### Transposing

To transpose a matrix, **swap its rows & cols**.

### Multiplication

```math
c_{ij} = A.\text{row}[i] \cdot B.\text{col}[j]
```

- Associative: $A(BC) = (AB)C$
- Distributes over addition: $A(B + C) = AB + AC$
- NOT commutative: $AB \ne BA$
- $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$
- $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$

### Matrix inversion

We won't have to compute a matrix inverse, but we will use inverses.

```math
MM^{-1} = M^{-1}M = I
```

## Row vs. Col vectors

The important difference btwn row vs. col vectors for us is the order in which you mtx-mult for applying transformations. For a col vector $\mathbf v$:

```math
CBA\mathbf v = \mathbf v^T ABC
```
